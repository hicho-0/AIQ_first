GIN — Graph Isomorphism Network

Sum-based aggregation + MLP — more expressive than GCN

How GIN processes your sensor graph

h_i⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾ = MLP⁽ˡ⁾( (1+ε)·h_i⁽ˡ⁾ + Σⱼ∈N(i) h_j⁽ˡ⁾ )

GIN SUMS neighbor embeddings instead of averaging. This preserves structural information: 3 activated sensors produce 3× the signal, unlike GCN where they look identical to 1 very-activated sensor. The MLP applies a learned nonlinear transformation.

Why GIN is better for detecting SO₂↔NO₂↔O₃ co-activation

During fire events, all three pollutant gases spike simultaneously. GCN averages them — a group of 3 nodes looks the same as 1 very activated node. GIN SUMS them — 3 activated sensors produce 3× stronger signal. This makes GIN far more sensitive to multi-sensor pollution patterns.

Regression Metrics — Model Evaluation

Predicting air pollutant concentrations (PM2.5, NO₂, O₃, AQI) using GNN, LSTM, XGBoost

MAE

1/n · Σ|yᵢ − ŷᵢ|

Average absolute error in same units as sensor (µg/m³). Easy to interpret practically.

RMSE

√(1/n · Σ(yᵢ−ŷᵢ)²)

Penalizes large errors heavily. Critical for catching missed pollution spikes and anomalies.

R²

1 − SS_res/SS_tot

Explains variance. 1.0=perfect. 0.9+=excellent. 0.7-0.9=good. Below 0.5=weak.

MAPE

100/n · Σ|yᵢ−ŷᵢ|/|yᵢ|

Percentage error. Useful for non-technical stakeholders. Avoid when values near zero.

Conclusion — which model is best for indoor pollution detection?

GCN — Baseline

R² ~0.80. Fast training, mean aggregation on correlation graph. Good starting point. Limitation: misses multi-sensor co-activation patterns without deep stacking.

LSTM — Sequential

R² ~0.86. Captures temporal dynamics well. Good for time-series forecasting. Does not exploit spatial relationships between sensors — treats each independently.

GNN (GraphSAT) — Best

R² ~0.94. Global attention learns SO₂↔NO₂↔O₃ pollution signature. Excellent on spikes (RMSE=2.75). Recommended for production air quality monitoring system.
